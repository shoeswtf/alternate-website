Your right foot, from above

01

Start with the bones

Each foot is a scaffold of 26 bones in three groups: the rearfoot (heel and ankle), the midfoot (the arch), and the forefoot (the long rays and your toes). A last — the wooden form every shoe is built on — is a portrait of these bones. Get them right and the leather disappears; get them wrong and no amount of craftsmanship saves the shoe.

02

The talus — receiver of weight

The talus sits directly under your shin bones and hands your body’s entire load down into the heel. No muscle attaches to it; it is pure transmission.

What we note: how your ankle flexes over the talus, because it sets the heel heights you can comfortably wear.

03

The calcaneus — the heel

The largest bone in the foot and usually the first to land in every step. Its shape decides whether a heel counter cradles you or chews you up.

What we note: heel width and the squareness of the heel — we trace it, not guess it.

04

The navicular — arch keystone

A small boat-shaped bone on the inside of your midfoot. Its height above the floor is your arch height, and it rises and falls with every step.

What we note: navicular height standing versus seated — the difference tells us how much your arch moves.

05

The cuboid — outer buttress

On the little-toe side of the midfoot, the cuboid braces each push-off. It keeps the outer edge of the foot from collapsing.

What we note: how firm the sole should be under this bone — stability here keeps shoes from rolling inward.

06

The cuneiforms — three wedges

Three wedge-shaped bones route force from the midfoot out into the forefoot, with most of it travelling through the first ray under your big toe.

What we note: heel-to-ball length — if the ball of the shoe misses this joint line, even a correct length feels wrong.

07

The metatarsals — the ball

Five long bones whose heads form the ball of your foot. They splay wider when you stand and bear your full weight at toe-off.

What we measure: ball width and ball girth standing, never seated — the bones spread under load.

08

The phalanges — fourteen toes

Two for the big toe, three for each smaller toe. They grip, balance, and need somewhere to live.

What we allow for: toe-box depth and length — and remember, your longest toe isn’t always the big one.

Calipers, tapes & honesty

What we measure

Eight measurements per foot, taken standing where the bones have settled into their working shape. We fit the larger of your two feet — nobody’s feet are twins.

Have an orthopedist?

If you wear orthoses or your orthopedist has opinions about your footwear, have them contact us directly at {{ site.email }}. Orthotic inserts, lifts, and rocker soles are routine work for us — and we’d rather hear it from them than guess at it.

One visit, ninety minutes

How a fitting works

  1. 1

    The conversation

    We start sitting down: what you wear now, what hurts, what you’ve given up on. Shoes you love and shoes you never wear both teach us something.

  2. 2

    Barefoot examination

    Feet inspected, old calluses and pressure marks read like margin notes. Then all eight measurements, standing, both feet.

  3. 3

    Gait & arch read

    You walk a strip of paper and an ink mat while we watch heel strike, roll-through, and toe-off. The wet footprint test confirms your arch type.

  4. 4

    Last selection

    Made-to-measure means we pick the closest last from our library, then adjust it — grinding, building up, padding — until it matches your measurements. We don’t carve custom lasts; we fit ours to you.

  5. 5

    Try-on prototype

    Before good leather is cut, you try a stitched canvas mock-up over your last. We mark tight spots, loose spots, and anything your bones disagree with.

  6. 6

    Build, break-in & follow-up

    Your pair is made over the corrected last and delivered two to six weeks later. Come back after fifty miles for a free fit check and adjustment.
