Start with the bones

Each foot is a scaffold of 26 bones in three groups: the rearfoot (heel and ankle), the midfoot (the arch), and the forefoot (the long rays and your toes). A last — the wooden form every shoe is built on — is a portrait of these bones. Get them right and the leather disappears; get them wrong and no amount of craftsmanship saves the shoe.

Flip the diagram between the two views up there, and keep an eye on the coloured loops — each one marks where a measuring tape wraps.

02

The talus — receiver of weight

The talus sits directly under your shin bones and hands your body’s entire load down into the heel. No muscle attaches to it; it is pure transmission.

What we note: how your ankle flexes over the talus, because it sets the heel heights you can comfortably wear.

03

The calcaneus — the heel

The largest bone in the foot and usually the first to land in every step. Its shape decides whether a heel counter cradles you or chews you up.

What we note: heel width and the squareness of the heel — we trace it, not guess it.

04

The navicular — arch keystone

A small boat-shaped bone on the inside of your midfoot. Its height above the floor is your arch height, and it rises and falls with every step.

What we note: navicular height standing versus seated — the difference tells us how much your arch moves.

05

The cuboid — outer buttress

On the little-toe side of the midfoot, the cuboid braces each push-off. It keeps the outer edge of the foot from collapsing.

What we note: how firm the sole should be under this bone — stability here keeps shoes from rolling inward.

06

The cuneiforms — three wedges

Three wedge-shaped bones route force from the midfoot out into the forefoot, with most of it travelling through the first ray under your big toe.

What we note: heel-to-ball length — if the ball of the shoe misses this joint line, even a correct length feels wrong.

07

The metatarsals — the ball

Five long bones whose heads form the ball of your foot. They splay wider when you stand and bear your full weight at toe-off.

What we measure: ball width and ball girth standing, never seated — the bones spread under load.

08

The phalanges — fourteen toes

Two for the big toe, three for each smaller toe. They grip, balance, and need somewhere to live.

What we allow for: toe-box depth and length — and remember, your longest toe isn’t always the big one.

09

Angled ball girth — the tape crosses the knuckles

Tape slanted around the ball of your foot, taken square behind the first metatarsal head and swept toward the little toe. Shoemakers call this the angled ball girth; its square-on sibling is the straight ball girth. We record it standing — the heads spread under load.

What it sets: the volume of the forepart. Too tight here, and no amount of extra length saves the shoe.

10

Instep girth — over the crest

Tape around the waist of your foot, cresting over the instep where the cuneiforms sit. Every maker finds that crest a little differently — some feel for the groove between the first and second metatarsal — so we note exactly where ours was taken.

What it sets: how far apart your laces or strap can sit, and whether a vamp clears your midfoot at all.

11

Arch girth — the big loop

Tape around the arch and the cone — the part of your foot that rises toward the front of your ankle. Read together with the short heel, it maps how your leg stacks itself over your foot.

What it sets: the throat line of boots and high vamps — everything that has to clear this loop.

Calipers, tapes & honesty

What we measure

Centimetres, taken standing where the bones have settled into their working shape — taped onto marked paper strips so nothing gets rounded to a tidy number. The three loops you just saw give us the girths; these give us the frame. We fit the larger of your two feet — nobody’s feet are twins.

Above the ankle

Boots go higher

Once the leather climbs past your ankle bones, three more measurements decide whether the shaft moves with you or fights you.

Planning taller shafts? We’ll also take the ankle girth just above the ankle bone and calf girths at recorded heights above the heel — five, seven inches, or wherever your calf is fullest.

One visit, ninety minutes

How a fitting works

  1. 1

    The conversation

    We start sitting down: what you wear now, what hurts, what you’ve given up on. Shoes you love and shoes you never wear both teach us something. Morning slots measure best — feet are least swollen first thing.

  2. 2

    Tracing & measuring

    Seated, hips, knees and ankles at right angles. Each foot is traced on folder stock — pencil upright for the outline, angled to catch the bite of the arch. Then centimetres, on marked paper strips that record exactly where the tape sat: lengths, widths, heights, and every girth from the loops above. Then all of it again with legs crossed — ankles rotate, and heels move more than you’d think.

  3. 3

    Gait & pressure read

    You walk a strip of paper while we watch heel strike, roll-through, and toe-off. A wet footprint and a standing pedigraph — your weight-bearing edge inked and traced — confirm how your arch actually behaves under load. We fit what’s there, not what a size chart wishes were there.

  4. 4

    Last selection

    Made-to-measure means we pick the closest last from our library, then adjust it — grinding, building up, padding — until it matches your measurements. We don’t carve custom lasts; we fit ours to you.

  5. 5

    Try-on prototype

    Before good leather is cut, you try a stitched canvas mock-up over your last. We mark tight spots, loose spots, and anything your bones disagree with.

  6. 6

    Build, break-in & follow-up

    Your pair is made over the corrected last and delivered two to six weeks later. Come back after fifty miles for a free fit check and adjustment.
